If charging is slower than expected or does not start, open **Charging** and **Power**. These screens show what the car reports about the charging connection, battery limits and electrical system. They can help you narrow down the cause, although a missing reading cannot identify a fault by itself.

## 1. How AC and DC charging differ inside the car

Understanding which part of the car is in charge helps explain what you see on screen:

- **AC charging (home wallbox, destination charger, or domestic socket)**: Alternating current (AC) from the wall enters the car's **On-Board Charger (OBC)**. The OBC converts that AC power into high-voltage direct current (DC) to feed the battery. During AC charging, speed is limited by whichever of three things is lowest: the **cable's resistor rating**, the **wallbox pilot signal**, or the **car's own AC current limit** (set in the center screen or by the OBC's internal temperature).
- **DC fast charging (CCS public fast chargers)**: The external charger converts AC to high-voltage DC outside the car and connects straight to the battery pack through the fast-charge contactors. During DC fast charging, the **battery management computer (BECM)** continuously tells the station the maximum current and power the pack can safely accept based on its **State of Charge** and **cell temperature**.

## 2. What the Charging screen shows

### Connector, lock, and cable status
Before any power flows, the car checks the physical plug:

- **Plug detection (Proximity pin)**: Confirms whether a charging handle is physically seated in the charge port.
- **Charge-port lock actuator**: Shows whether the locking pin has engaged to secure the plug. (Many "charging fault" messages at public stations happen simply because a heavy cable pulled down on the handle and stopped the locking pin from sliding all the way in.)
- **Cable amp rating**: Charging cables contain a small resistor inside the plug that tells the car the maximum current the copper wire can safely carry (for example, 13 A, 16 A, 20 A, or 32 A).

### Live AC and DC electrical readings
- **AC input voltage, current, and frequency** coming from the wallbox into the On-Board Charger.
- **High-voltage DC output voltage, current, and power (kW)** flowing into the traction battery.
- **Charger and converter temperatures**: If an On-Board Charger gets too warm inside a hot garage, it smoothly dials back charging current to protect itself.

## 3. Plain-language charge-limitation diagnosis

Instead of making you compare half a dozen raw numbers by hand, the **Charging** screen runs a **charge-limitation check** that answers the question: *"What is currently capping my charging speed?"*

Depending on what the modules report, Hanterill highlights the limiting factor in plain language:

1. **Limited by the charging station or wallbox**: The wallbox's pilot signal is offering less current than the car and cable can accept (common on load-shared home chargers or busy public AC posts).
2. **Limited by the charging cable**: The cable plugged into the port is rated for lower current (for example, a 16 A cable used on a 32 A wallbox).
3. **Limited by an in-car current limit**: A user-configured AC amp cap is active in the car's center display.
4. **Limited by battery temperature (cold-gate)**: On DC fast charging, a cold battery pack cannot accept full fast-charge current without risking lithium plating on the cells. Once the battery heater warms the pack into its ideal window (`25 °C` to `35 °C`), the current limit rises.
5. **Limited by high State of Charge (the normal charge curve)**: Above roughly 50% to 60% State of Charge, every lithium-ion battery gradually steps down its maximum charging current as cell voltages approach their upper ceiling.

If a required sensor reading is unavailable or the car is not currently plugged into a charger, Hanterill states clearly that no active limit can be attributed rather than guessing.
