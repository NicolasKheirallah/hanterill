Hanterill talks to the car over Ethernet. A modern car is a small
network, and one of its plugs speaks ordinary internet. This page explains the cable, the
network, and what to do when the car does not appear.

## The cable

An ENET cable connects specific OBD-II pins to a standard RJ45 plug. It contains no electronics.
It is not an ELM327, not a J2534 interface, and not a Bluetooth adapter. The cable itself needs
no driver.

The chain looks like this:

```text
OBD-II port in the car
  -> ENET cable
  -> RJ45
  -> USB-C Ethernet adapter
  -> your laptop
```

Any passive OBD-II to Ethernet cable works, including the ones sold as "BMW ENET" or "E-Sys"
cables. They are the same wiring.

## How the car appears on your network

Cars that support this use IPv4 link-local addressing, the `169.254.x.x` range. It is the same
scheme two computers use when you plug a cable directly between them: each side picks an address
without needing a router.

Most of the time the car and your adapter sort this out on their own. If your operating system
sits waiting for a DHCP server that will never come, set the adapter to link-local or automatic
private addressing manually.

<Callout kind="note" title="What Hanterill actually does">
It sends a discovery broadcast on UDP port 13400, listens for a car to announce itself, opens a
TCP connection to that same port, and asks the car to activate a diagnostic route before sending
anything else. The [Architecture](/docs/architecture) page covers each step.
</Callout>

Hanterill picks the network interface for you. It ranks every adapter it can see, ignores virtual
ones like VPNs and loopback, and prefers a wired link-local address. If you plug the cable in or
out mid-session, it notices without needing a restart.

## If the car does not appear

Work down this list. The first two cover most cases:

- Is the car at ignition-on? A sleeping car answers nothing.
- Is the RJ45 end in an Ethernet port, not a USB-only port?
- Is a VPN or firewall blocking UDP 13400? Try disabling them briefly.
- Try another Ethernet adapter. Some cheap USB adapters drop link-local traffic.
- Is another diagnostic tool holding the connection? Only one tool can talk to the car at a time.

<Callout kind="caution" title="One cable, one car">
The cable goes from your computer to the car's diagnostic port, never to your home router. Do not
bridge the car's network to your normal network.
</Callout>

## When the link drops

Link recovery is built into the transport, so a brief interruption does not cost you the session:

- If the TCP connection drops, Hanterill re-activates the route on the surviving link.
- Replies from the old connection are rejected as stale, so a late answer cannot be mistaken for a
  fresh one.
- A keepalive (`TesterPresent` every couple of seconds) stops the car from putting modules to sleep
  in the middle of a long scan.
- If the car itself reports a different identity after a reconnect, Hanterill ends the session
  rather than mixing two cars' readings into one file.

## Wireless

Some cars expose diagnostics over their own Wi-Fi access point. If that is available and Hanterill
supports it, discovery and routing work the same way over the wireless interface. The wired path is
the tested one.
