The network explorer is the wiring view of the CMA electrical architecture. It
draws every control unit, the typed links between them and the pin rows the
diagnostics run over, as one interactive map. Use it to see where a module sits,
which network it hangs on, and which connector pins carry the link. It is
reference documentation, not a reading from the car.

## What the map is

The map is built from one independent CMA wiring study, not from a live
session. It holds 170 units, 295 typed links across nine networks, and 622
documented pin rows, with 170 bus messages captured alongside them. The
study's own site disclaimer is literal: this is enthusiast documentation and it
is not affiliated with or endorsed by a vehicle manufacturer.

Hanterill's own read path is narrower. The app addresses a module by its DoIP
logical address and sends a request. The map names a module by its connector
reference and shows every wire that reaches it. A reader can move between the
two, but they are separate descriptions of the same car.

## Reading the canvas

The canvas groups units into domain clusters: drivetrain and high voltage,
chassis and braking, ADAS and safety, body and comfort, driver controls,
displays and infotainment, connectivity and diagnostics, core computers, power
distribution, and a peripheral band for sensors, switches and actuators. Links
run along corridors between the clusters, so a long run is readable and a
crossing is a real route, not a drawing shortcut.

Three controls do most of the work:

A click opens a detail drawer for the unit: its part number, its location in the
car, and the pin-out table where the study documents one. A unit without a
documented pin-out still shows the connections other pin-outs imply.

The segments carry the physical detail the summary hides: bus speed, wire
colours for the high and low pair, the modules named in the study's segment
list, and a message count where the study recorded one.

## DoIP and the CAN segments

Hanterill reaches a CMA car over DoIP, which is diagnostics over IP. On this
architecture that traffic rides the OBD Ethernet segment between the vehicle
gateway module and the OBD connector. The map shows it as one Ethernet link,
and it is the link the app uses.

The CAN segments are the older path. A CAN bus carries the same UDS questions
inside ISO-TP frames, and the diagnostics and connectivity CAN segment is the
one wired to the OBD connector. Hanterill implements an ISO-TP transport, but
it ships no CAN adapter, so that path only works with hardware you supply.
[Connection](/docs/connection) covers what each link needs, and
[Architecture](/docs/architecture) describes the DoIP envelope and the ISO-TP
fallback in more detail.

## The two gateways

Two modules sit between the OBD connector and the rest of the car, and the map
marks both in the core cluster.

<SpecList rows={[
  ["CEM at 4/56", "Central electronic module, the car's central body computer. It sits on the FlexRay backbone and appears on several CAN and LIN segments"],
  ["VGM at 4/314", "Vehicle gateway module. It carries the OBD Ethernet link and the infotainment CAN segment"],
]} />

The FlexRay backbone is the shortest way to read the core of the car: the CEM,
the VGM, the driver information module, the infotainment head unit and the
vehicle control unit all appear on that one segment. When you trace a path from
a cabin module to a drivetrain module, it usually crosses a gateway rather than
running point to point.

## Catalogue rows and live registry

The map and Hanterill's ECU registry answer different questions, and the counts
make the difference clear. The map is the physical view: 170 units, including
sensors, switches, actuators and power distribution. The
[ECU reference](/docs/ecu-reference) is the addressing view: 49 CMA rows that
Hanterill can talk to over DoIP, each with a logical address.

Of those 49 rows, 44 were observed on a live battery-electric car and five are
reference entries that have never answered. Those five, TCM, NRCM, SUM, DEM and
MVBM, are positions a hybrid or air-suspension car would populate. On the
electric reference car they stay silent, and Hanterill records the silence as
expected.

Two consequences for reading the map. First, the map is wider than the
registry, so a unit on the canvas is not automatically a module the app can
address, and vice versa. Second, the five hybrid-only reference rows do not
appear as named units on the map at all. The map is not derived from Hanterill's
registry, and the registry is not derived from the map.

<Callout kind="note" title="The same label can mean two things">
The map names a unit ENGINE CONTROL MODULE ECM. On the battery-electric
reference car the module at address `0x1630` answered as a drive-unit
controller, so the app's ECM row describes that behaviour, not an engine
controller. A label on a diagram and a live answer are two different claims.
</Callout>

## Pins, notes and inferred links

Solid lines on the canvas are documented pin-outs: the study traced the wire
and recorded the pin, the net and the wire colour. Dashed lines are inferred
from the block diagram, where the connection is clear but no pin row was
captured. The distinction is there so you never read a hypothesis as a
continuity check.

Open a unit to get its pin table. Each row names a target, a net, a pin number,
the wire colour and any note the study attached. From a pin you can jump to the
net and to the module at the other end.

## What the map does not say

The map does not carry DoIP logical addresses, so it cannot tell you which
module answers a request. It does not carry software versions, fault codes or
live values, because it is wiring documentation rather than a diagnostic
reading. It also does not describe a specific car's build. Options and markets
differ, and a link on the map may not be populated on the car in front of you.

[Architecture](/docs/architecture) covers how Hanterill uses the gateway, and
the [ECU reference](/docs/ecu-reference) lists the addresses the app actually
talks to.
